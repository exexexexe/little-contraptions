Rain Sound Machine

Whatever is actually falling where you are, turned into a sound. Nothing here is a recording — every drop is made up out of noise and a filter at the moment you hear it.

The reading is real and the sound is not a recording. Your rough position comes from the same keyless /api/where the desktop's weather widget uses — city level at best, nothing stored — and the weather itself from Open-Meteo, which needs no key. Weather data by Open-Meteo.com, used under CC BY 4.0. The panel above prints the actual millimetres and the actual WMO code rather than a word chosen to match the sound.

How millimetres become a noise. The intensity bands are the standard meteorological ones, not invented: under 2.5 mm/h is light, 2.5–7.6 is moderate, and over 7.6 is heavy. That number sets five layers, all generated here: a wide band of filtered noise for the hiss of many drops at once; a mid band around 600 Hz for the body of it; a low brown-noise rumble that only comes in when it is really coming down; individual droplet impacts, scheduled at a rate that follows the intensity, each a few milliseconds of noise through a sharp resonant filter; and the occasional slower plink of water falling into water that has already collected.

When it is not raining you get a clear-sky hum instead — two sine tones a fraction apart, beating slowly against each other, under a breath of filtered air. An empty panel would be the honest thing to show and a bad thing to listen to. Thunder is only ever armed when the real WMO code is one of the thunderstorm ones (95, 96, 99), so you will not get a storm that is not happening.

The slider moves the sound, never the reading. Wherever you push it, the panel goes on saying what is actually falling on you.
