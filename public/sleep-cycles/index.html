The Night, Counted in Cycles

Sleep does not run out evenly. It comes in rounds, and it is kinder to be woken between them than in the middle of one.

A schematic of the shape a night tends to take — not a recording of yours, and not a prediction. Deep sleep clusters in the early cycles; the dreaming stretches get longer towards morning.

Where the ninety minutes comes from. A sleep cycle runs from light sleep down through deep, slow-wave sleep and back up into REM. In adults it averages roughly ninety minutes, but the honest figure is a range — commonly given as about seventy to a hundred and twenty — and it varies between people and across a single night. Early cycles carry more deep sleep and later ones more REM, so they are not even interchangeable with each other.

Why the end of a cycle is the kind place to wake. The grogginess of being hauled out of slow-wave sleep is a real, named thing: sleep inertia. It is worst when you wake from deep sleep and mildest from the light sleep that ends a cycle. That is the whole idea behind this arithmetic.

What this page cannot do. It has no idea when your cycles actually turn over. It is multiplying a population average by a whole number and adding it to a clock. Treat it as a nudge towards a sensible bedtime, not as a measurement — and if you are reliably exhausted, the answer is a doctor, not a calculator.

Sleep-stage architecture and the ninety-minute average are standard sleep-medicine description; the seven-to-nine-hour figure for adults is the common public health recommendation. Nothing on this page is measured, and nothing you type here leaves your browser.
